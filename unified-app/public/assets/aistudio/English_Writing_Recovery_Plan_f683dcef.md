# English Writing Recovery Plan

Nov 2, 2026 · @King

Six months at about 30 minutes a day, in four phases: restart sentences (weeks 1–4), control paragraphs (weeks 5–12), finish whole pieces (months 4–6), then publish once a month. Your writing ability is out of practice, not gone, and only unaided writing brings it back.

You move to the next phase when its gate is met, not when the calendar says so. If a phase runs long, the plan is still working.

## The rules

You write every word yourself; AI only points at problems after the draft is done. In a small MIT study (54 students), people who drafted essays with ChatGPT for three sessions struggled to recall and engage when they later wrote without it. People who wrote unaided first kept that advantage once they got the tool. Treat it as a warning, not proof: the sample is small and short-term.

| Use | Allowed | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI drafts or rewrites any sentence | Never | This is what caused the loss |
| Grammarly or autocomplete while drafting | Off | Suggestions interrupt the sentence you are building |
| AI marks errors after the draft, without fixing them | Yes | You still do the repair, which is the learning |
| AI explains a grammar rule you got wrong | Yes | Rules stick when tied to your own error |
| Dictionary or example sentences for one word | Yes | Checks usage without writing for you |

**Time budget.** Writing is a side goal behind your MSc and your job, so the plan is sized to fit beside them:

- 25 minutes of writing, 5 days a week.
- 15 minutes of reading in English every day, in place of phone scrolling if possible.
- One 60-minute session at the weekend, from Phase 2.

Assume you will miss about a third of sessions. The plan still works at 4 sessions a week; it fails at zero for two weeks.

**Anchor it.** Write one line before you start: "When I \[existing daily routine\], I will write for 25 minutes at \[place\]." A fixed cue beats waiting for free time.

## Phase 1 — Restart (weeks 1–4)

The goal is to write plain, correct sentences again without freezing. Quality does not matter yet; volume and speed do.

**The daily 25-minute session**

1. **Copy (5 min).** Copy one paragraph by hand from a clear writer, such as a [Paul Graham essay](https://paulgraham.com/articles.html). Copying puts native sentence patterns back in your hand without the pressure of inventing content.
2. **Write (15 min).** Write without stopping about something you know well: your day, a board you debugged, how the Hub handles a booking. Do not delete and do not run a checker. Familiar topics free your attention for the sentences themselves.
3. **Check (5 min).** Read it aloud. Underline 3 problems yourself and add them to the tracker.

**Sentence rules for this phase**

- One idea per sentence.
- Subject and verb within the first five words.
- 10–20 words per sentence.
- When stuck, write the simplest true sentence ("The board did not boot.") and build from it.

**Once a week,** send me one piece. I name each error without rewriting it, and you fix it.

**Gate to move on**

- [ ] 15 sessions done
- [ ] 150 words written in 15 minutes without stopping
- [ ] Your 4–5 most frequent errors identified in the tracker

My estimate, not a research figure: sentence fluency usually feels noticeably better after 2–3 weeks of daily writing.

## Phase 2 — Control (weeks 5–12)

The goal is to control paragraphs on purpose, not by luck. Each weekday gets one drill, and each drill trains one part of the skill. The mix follows the methods that ranked highest for writing quality in the *Writing Next* review: planning and revising strategies, summarizing, sentence combining, and studying models.

| Day | Drill (25 min) | What it trains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | Franklin rebuild, part 1: take a 150-word model paragraph, write a 3–5 word note for each sentence, put the original away | Separating ideas from wording |
| Tue | Sentence combining: join 6 short sentences into 2–3 longer ones, three different ways | Sentence variety and control |
| Wed | Franklin rebuild, part 2: rebuild Monday's paragraph from the notes only, then compare it with the original and note 2 differences | Word choice and structure, learned from a model |
| Thu | Summary: 100 words on an article you read this week | Finding and stating the main point |
| Fri | One paragraph with the 6 steps: point, notes, order, draft, link, close | Planning and structure |
| Weekend (60 min) | One 400–600 word piece: plan and draft on Saturday, revise on Sunday | Whole-piece planning and revision |

**Franklin's variation.** Every second week, shuffle Monday's notes before Wednesday's rebuild and put them back in order yourself. Franklin said this taught him "method in the arrangement of thoughts."

**Weekend revision checklist**

- The point is in the first sentence of each paragraph.
- Each sentence picks up something from the one before it.
- No sentence runs past 25 words.
- The opening states a problem (with *but*, *however* or *although*) and what it costs, not background.
- It reads aloud without stumbling.

**Gate to move on**

- [ ] 6 Franklin rebuilds in which your version keeps every idea of the original
- [ ] 8 weekend pieces finished and revised
- [ ] Your top 3 errors from Phase 1 appear at most once per 300 words

## Phase 3 — Finished pieces (months 4–6)

The goal is to finish whole pieces that a stranger would read to the end. Pick one form for these three months and stay with it.

| Form | Length | Good fit if | Study |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Essays or blog posts | 800–1,500 words | You want to explain ideas or what you learned building things | Paul Graham's essays; Zinsser, *On Writing Well* |
| Short stories | 1,500–3,000 words | You want characters, scenes and dialogue | Le Guin, *Steering the Craft* (built around exercises) |

If you can't decide, start with essays. You already have the material from engineering, the Hub and what you are learning, and the same skills carry into your thesis.

**Two-week cycle**

1. **Week A:** plan and draft the piece in your weekday sessions.
2. **Week B:** revise it, post it for critique, then revise again from the feedback.
3. Keep 2 weekday drills (a Franklin rebuild on a model from your chosen form, and sentence combining). Use the other 3 days for the piece.

**Get outside readers.** Join one critique community. [Scribophile](https://www.scribophile.com) works on points: you critique other members' work to earn critiques of your own. Writing those critiques is training too, because spotting problems in other people's drafts sharpens your own revision. If you need company to keep the habit, [London Writers' Salon](https://londonwriterssalon.com) runs online writing sprints (paid).

**Gate to move on**

- [ ] 3 finished pieces of 800+ words
- [ ] Each revised after feedback from at least 2 readers
- [ ] 5 critiques written for other writers

## Phase 4 — Spare-time writer (month 7 on)

From here you are a writer who publishes once a month. Choose one public place and stay with it: a Substack newsletter, Medium, LinkedIn articles, or a blog for the Hub.

**Monthly rhythm**

- Weeks 1–2: draft the month's piece.
- Week 3: get critique and revise.
- Week 4: final read-aloud pass, then publish.
- Every week: one Franklin rebuild on a writer you admire in your form, to keep learning from models.

Set expectations honestly. Research on writing expertise puts real mastery at a decade or more of deliberate practice, so improvement continues for years. That is normal, and it is not a reason to wait: publishing regularly is how the next stage of practice happens.

## Reading list

One book per phase is enough. Read it in your 15-minute daily reading slot, and on other days read anything in English you enjoy. Reading volume matters on its own: research reviews find that reading improves writing quality.

| Phase | Book | Why this one |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Verlyn Klinkenborg, *Several Short Sentences About Writing* | Teaches writing one sentence at a time, which is exactly where you are starting |
| 2 | Joseph M. Williams and Joseph Bizup, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* | The clearest guide to why some sentences are easy to read and others are not |
| 3 (essays) | William Zinsser, *On Writing Well* | The standard guide to clear nonfiction |
| 3 (stories) | Ursula K. Le Guin, *Steering the Craft* | Short chapters, each with exercises to do |
| 4 | Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* | Explains why expert writers lose their readers and how to fix it |
